# ACT Compendium 2026 (Internal Draft)

Internal working draft. Not for distribution. Partner references require review. [INTERNAL ONLY]

## One-liner

A regenerative innovation studio stewarding a working farm on Jinibara Country.

## 1. Identity and intent

ACT exists to grow co-stewardship and shared governance through land, story, and action. The farm is held as a commons. The studio is the toolkit communities can hold, shape, and outgrow. We work to dismantle extractive systems through ethical technology, Indigenous data sovereignty, and community-centred design.

This compendium anchors 2026 decisions in LCAA and balances land practice with innovation, enterprise, and product work so we speak with one voice across projects. ALMA is the core impact and learning model; Empathy Ledger is the core impact tool. Consented voices anchor the narrative wherever possible. It guides internal writing, development, partnerships, and delivery.

### A practice book, not a report

This compendium is a field guide, written to be used: on Country, in the studio, in meetings, and in the quiet hours where decisions are made. It will never be finished. We add stories, prototypes, drawings, and learnings as we go. A good page helps someone act with care.

### The ACT way: make space

"The best systems are invisible. They create space for humans to be human."

We build tractors and toolkits, not empires. Infrastructure should disappear into the work, make listening easier, keep consent visible, and leave more time for people to gather, make, and lead.

### Identity before product

We start with identity and belonging, then build the thing. Events are culture, not marketing. Communities are owners, not audiences.

## 2. How to use this compendium

- Align project plans, product choices, and communications with LCAA.
- Keep land practice and cultural sovereignty central as enterprise grows.
- Coordinate across projects without flattening their identities.
- Keep claims honest and grounded in lived practice.
- Use it as a living record: add stories, shifts, and open questions as they arise.

## 3. Method: LCAA

LCAA is our operating rhythm. It is not a marketing frame. It is how we choose, build, and learn. Think of it as a story arc we return to.

- Listen: Start with place, people, history, and community voice. We do not assume. We ask.
- Curiosity: Ask better questions, prototype, test, and learn together.
- Action: Build with partners and deliver tangible outcomes that can be owned locally.
- Art: Translate change into culture, meaning, and story.

LCAA loops. Art returns us to Listen.

## 4. Principles of practice (the spine)

These principles are the backbone of the compendium, not slogans or values posters. They are choices we make in rooms, in code, on Country, and in how we show up. When we are unclear, we return here. Each principle links land practice, enterprise, story, and technology into one way of working.

### 1. Country sets the pace

The land is the teacher and the limiter. We do not scale beyond land capacity or community readiness. Conservation-first is the baseline. If the creek is low or the land is tired, we slow down and listen.

We plan residencies, harvest shares, and enterprise activity to seasons and capacity. If a project asks for more than the land can give, we redesign the project, not the land.

### 2. Community authority comes first

Consent, cultural protocols, and local authority are non-negotiable. OCAP is enforced in code and practice. Elder review is required where culture demands it. Community authority carries the highest weight, even when it complicates delivery.

Authority governs data and images. If Elders say wait, we wait. If a partner wants speed over safety, we slow down.

### 3. Identity before product

We start with belonging, not features. Events are culture, not marketing. A gathering counts as an output if it strengthens trust and shared governance. We would rather build slower with the right people than fast with the wrong assumptions.

The Harvest is not just a venue; it is belonging tested in markets, meals, and shared work. Products are valid only if they strengthen that belonging.

### 4. Build for handover (Beautiful Obsolescence)

We design for transfer from day one. Documentation, training, and governance are deliverables. A Curious Tractor means tools can be repaired locally, adapted by communities, and left behind. Success is measured by how well we step back.

Handover is planned. We write playbooks, train caretakers, and build maintenance loops so the work can live without us.

> Field note: If we cannot hand it over, we are still in Curiosity.

### 5. Make with lived experience

Lived experience is not advisory; it is core capability. We build pathways for people under-skilled in formal systems but rich in lived knowledge. We hire, train, and design around those who carry the truth of the problem.

This includes real roles in manufacturing, recycling, maintenance, and storytelling. We pay for time, design for dignity, and do not treat lived experience as free labour.

### 6. Enterprise funds the commons

Goods, Harvest, and related enterprise fund land care and community value, not extraction. Fair value return and community ownership are non-negotiable. Enterprise keeps the commons alive.

We keep enterprise small enough to be cared for, and strong enough to fund land care, community wages, and long-term repair.

### 7. Evidence is story, not surveillance

Empathy Ledger is the core impact tool. ALMA is the shared learning lens. We do not profile people. We track system-level signals, learn from consented stories, and change practice in response.

Metrics support this, but do not lead it. Stories are evidence because they carry context, harm risk, and authority.

> Field note: We do not measure people. We measure whether the system is learning.

### 8. Tools should create space

Infrastructure should be quiet. The Knowledge Hub and Farmhand reduce admin, hold context, and make it easier to do the work that matters. The system is the tractor, the humans are the farmers, the community is the harvest.

The Knowledge Hub is shared memory, not surveillance. Farmhand should never override community voice; it should make it easier to hear.

### 9. Share with care

We share what is safe and keep what is sensitive. The shareability matrix is not bureaucracy; it is respect in practice. If a story cannot be told without harm, we do not tell it. We wait and listen again.

External sharing requires clarity about consent scope, cultural sensitivity, and purpose. We do not trade story for attention.

### 10. Art returns us to Listen

Art is not a layer on top. It is how we make change felt and understood. Art brings us back to listening and keeps the work human. The LCAA loop only completes when Art returns us to Listen.

Residencies, exhibitions, and installations are not endpoints; they are invitations back into practice.

These principles are how we keep the work grounded as it grows. We will revisit and revise them as community authority and land practice evolve.

## 5. Place and land practice (Black Cockatoo Valley)

Black Cockatoo Valley is a 150-acre property on Jinibara Country near Witta, Queensland. Conservation-first, we work with Country, not on it. The land sets the pace; we do not scale beyond what the land and community can carry.

Key commitments:

- Low-impact, limited-volume activity. No extractive tourism.
- Residencies and workshops fund restoration, weed management, and monitoring.
- Revenue is reinvested into habitat care and stewardship.
- The long-term aim is community co-stewardship and beautiful obsolescence.

2026 shift:

- Balance land management with studio innovation, product development, and enterprise delivery.
- Run The Harvest in Witta as a small, practical enterprise hub.
- Push Goods toward the next level of manufacturing and operational maturity.
- Treat stories and technology as core instruments of stewardship, not add-ons.

Land practice remains central alongside innovation and enterprise. Every project should explain how it strengthens land practice and advances responsible innovation.

> Field note: When the creek is low or the grass is tired, we slow down. Capacity is a land decision before it is a calendar decision.

## 7. A Curious Tractor and Beautiful Obsolescence

A Curious Tractor names ACT's platform expression. It is how we build tools communities can own, adapt, and outgrow. Think of it as the tractor power take-off: shared capacity that can attach to many implements, then be unhitched, repaired locally, and left behind.

Beautiful Obsolescence means:

- We design for handover, not dependency.
- Communities own their data, stories, and decisions in the work we do together.
- Systems are designed to be forkable and transferable.
- Our success is measured by how well we can step back.

This is not a brand line. It is a delivery discipline that shapes architecture, documentation, training, and governance.

> Field note: Documentation and training are deliverables, not extras. If we cannot hand it over, we have not finished the work.

## 8. 2026 focus areas

This year is about balance: land stewardship, studio innovation, and enterprise maturity moving in step.

1. Land stewardship with shared governance

- Keep conservation-first practice as the baseline.
- Prepare co-stewardship groundwork (relationship, protocol, readiness).
- Scale land programs to land capacity and community readiness.

2. Innovation studio and product work

- Build tools that serve community ownership, not dependence.
- Prioritise clarity, simplicity, and handover readiness.
- Use stories and evidence to guide product direction.

3. The Harvest as a grounded enterprise (Witta)

- The Harvest is an old nursery in Witta, now a small enterprise hub.
- Support local makers and micro-enterprise through markets, workshops, and venue hire.
- Build community connection through practical enterprise, shared learning, and testing.
- Prioritise sustainable, community-led, innovation-ready enterprise.

4. Goods on Country manufacturing

- Move Goods from pilots to reliable production.
- Strengthen supply chains, quality, and local capability.
- Hold community ownership and fair value return as non-negotiables.

5. Story and evidence

- Treat storytelling as infrastructure for change, not marketing.
- Protect consent and cultural protocols in every output.
- Use evidence to learn, not to extract.
- Anchor impact learning in ALMA and Empathy Ledger.

> Field note: Pace is a form of ethics. We move at the speed of trust, maintenance, and land capacity.

## 10. Infrastructure and operations

Global infrastructure keeps the ecosystem visible and coordinated. It should be quiet, reduce admin, and make stewardship easier. The ACT Knowledge Hub is the single source of truth (Notion command centre, codebases, Empathy Ledger) and should be explainable in five minutes.

Key elements:

- Shared automation across repos (daily syncs, reporting).
- Notion as the operating layer for sprints, projects, and reporting.
- Data flow that keeps external inputs visible without centralising control.

Principle: infrastructure serves the work, not the other way around.

Operational threads (internal):

- Knowledge capture: weekly updates to Notion with project slugs and ALMA signals.
- Media governance: Empathy Ledger is the source of truth for stories, photos, and video.
- Product infrastructure: act-global-infrastructure provides shared tooling, CI, and release discipline.
- Data hygiene: clear ownership, versioning, and consent flags on every record.
- Quiet reliability: fewer tools, clearer pathways, faster handover.

> Field note: If a system is loud, it is not doing its job. Quiet systems create room for people to show up.

## 11. Intelligence and ethics (Farmhand + ALMA)

Farmhand and ALMA are internal tools for coordination and learning. ALMA is the core impact and learning model across the ecosystem. Farmhand turns messy inputs into clear next actions across the Knowledge Hub. They matter only if they enforce cultural sovereignty and avoid extraction.

Non-negotiables:

- Cultural sovereignty is sacred; OCAP is enforced in code and practice.
- We observe systems, never optimise people.
- Sacred knowledge is hard-blocked, not warned.
- Community authority carries the highest weight in decision paths.

Farmhand role (internal):

- Turn messy inputs into clear tasks and next actions.
- Protect consent and cultural boundaries in summaries and outputs.
- Connect LCAA steps to real work without inflating claims.
- Keep ALMA signals visible without profiling individuals.

> Field note: Intelligence without consent is just extraction. We would rather be slower than wrong.

## 12. Core impact and learning model (ALMA + Empathy Ledger)

ALMA (Adaptive Learning for Meaningful Accountability) is our shared impact-and-learning model. It helps us notice system-level patterns without profiling people and keeps community authority central. ALMA is a way of paying attention, not scoring.

Empathy Ledger holds consented stories, community voice, and evidence that feeds learning across projects.

How this works in practice:

- Community stories live in Empathy Ledger with consent controls.
- ALMA reads signals at a system level, not an individual level.
- Learning feeds back into LCAA and informs product and enterprise choices.

ALMA data anchors (internal):

- Story record (Empathy Ledger ID)
- Consent scope and cultural sensitivity level
- Project slugs and place/Country
- ALMA signal tags and LCAA stage

ALMA in reporting:

- Story: a consented narrative or quote from Empathy Ledger.
- Signal: ALMA lens notes at system level (authority, harm risk, readiness, option value).
- Shift: what we changed or will change in practice (LCAA step).
- Scope: what is shareable now vs internal-only.

> Field note: If a story cannot be told without harm, we do not tell it. The signal stays internal until it is safe.

## 13. AI agent strategy aligned to ALMA

AI agents must align with ALMA and Empathy Ledger consent. The point is not automation at all costs, but ethical intelligence that protects community sovereignty.

Strategy:

- ALMA gatekeeps impact learning and ethics checks.
- Farmhand orchestrates tasks and uses ALMA to prevent extraction.
- Story support tools assist, never replace, community voice.
- AI can assist with synthesis and early diagrams; final narratives and visuals require human and community review.
- Voice and content tools (ACT Voice) must pass alignment checks and never override lived experience.
- No agent can profile, rank, or optimise individuals.

Agent roles (internal):

- Scribe: summarises meetings and captures next actions.
- Cartographer: maps relationships between projects, stories, and signals.
- Evidence steward: checks consent scope and shareability before release.
- Studio assistant: supports drafting and editing, never owns the narrative.

> Field note: The best AI output is the one that gets out of the way and lets the right voice be heard.

## 14. Governance and consent

Consent is not a compliance box; it is part of the work and the product. Community authority is the default, not the exception.

Commitments:

- Consent is explicit, ongoing, and revocable.
- Elder review is required where cultural protocols demand it.
- Sacred knowledge is protected by hard blocks.
- Partner-specific nuance stays internal until reviewed.

Shareability matrix (internal):

- INTERNAL ONLY: working notes, partner nuance, or sensitive detail.
- EXTERNAL-LITE: system-level learnings without identifiers or private details.
- EXTERNAL: explicit consent, cultural review, and safe-to-share assets.

> Field note: Consent is a relationship, not a checkbox. We keep listening after we share.

## 15. Evidence and learning

ALMA sets the learning lens, and Empathy Ledger holds consented evidence. Story is primary evidence; metrics only support learning.

How we learn:

- Capture consented stories in Empathy Ledger.
- Read system-level signals via ALMA (not individual profiles).
- Feed learning back into LCAA decisions and enterprise design.
- Share learnings with communities first, not last.

ALMA capture prompts (for teams):

- What did we hear, and who holds authority?
- What system-level shift is emerging?
- What harm risk or consent boundary must be held?
- What practical change do we commit to next?
- What should remain internal for now?

ALMA signal families (internal lens):

- Evidence strength
- Community authority
- Harm risk (inverted)
- Implementation capability
- Option value
- Community value return

> Field note: We count what we can, but we listen to what we cannot. Evidence serves relationships, not reports.

## 18. Risks and dependencies

Naming risk early is part of stewardship. We do not hide it in the margins.

- Capacity risk: small team carrying multi-project delivery.
- Consent risk: misuse of stories or data without proper review.
- Enterprise risk: supply chain and operational reliability in Goods and Harvest.
- Land risk: overextension beyond land or community readiness.
- AI risk: misalignment or extractive outputs.

Mitigation is discipline: fewer priorities, deeper listening, and clear guardrails.

> Field note: The risk we ignore is the one that shows up in the work.

## 19. Ways of working (internal)

Our way of working is part of the output. How we show up is the first signal of trust.

- Keep language grounded, non-extractive, and honest. No savior framing.

- Mark high-nuance partner details as [INTERNAL ONLY].

> Field note: The process is the product. If it is not respectful, the outcome will not be either.
