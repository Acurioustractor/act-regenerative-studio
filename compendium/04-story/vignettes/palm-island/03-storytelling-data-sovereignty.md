# Storytelling, Data Sovereignty, and Community Recovery

> "Our stories were always taken off the island. Reports went to Townsville, Brisbane, Canberra. We never saw them again. Now we're keeping them here, where they belong."

---

## Story Summary

Palm Island has a complex history with outside organizations collecting data and stories. Generations of researchers, journalists, and government agencies have arrived, extracted what they needed, and left—often with narratives that didn't reflect how the community saw itself.

When Cyclone Kirrily hit in early 2024, the community faced another wave of outsiders wanting to document the damage. But this time, the Palm Island Community Company (PICC) took a different approach. Instead of letting external photographers and journalists control the narrative, they partnered with ACT to build community capacity for their own story capture.

The Storm Stories project trained local community members in photography, videography, and interview techniques. More importantly, it established local protocols for who could tell what stories, and under what conditions those stories could leave the island.

Elders played a central role in defining consent boundaries. Some stories were for community healing only—never to be shared externally. Others could be shared with specific partners. A few were cleared for public advocacy. The Empathy Ledger consent architecture made these distinctions technically enforceable, not just policy documents that could be ignored.

The shift wasn't just technical—it was cultural. Young people who had grown up seeing their community portrayed negatively in media now held cameras and recording equipment. They were deciding how Palm Island would be seen.

Data sovereignty on Palm Island isn't abstract. It's the difference between a story being filed in a government database and a story being shared at a community gathering, with the storyteller present, deciding what happens next.
