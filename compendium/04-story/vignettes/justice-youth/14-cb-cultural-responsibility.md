# CB: Understanding Cultural Responsibility and Leadership

> *Quote withheld for identity protection*

---

## Story Summary

**⚠️ INTERNAL ONLY - This vignette describes a young person's cultural development journey and must remain confidential to protect their identity and cultural safety.**

CB's story is about discovering what it means to carry cultural responsibility. For many young First Nations people disconnected from community, culture is something that belongs to others—Elders, family members, the "real" Aboriginal people who know ceremony and language. The idea that they themselves might be custodians of cultural knowledge feels impossible.

Through the Oonchiumpa program, CB began a different journey. It started with questions:
- What does my family name mean?
- Where did my grandparents come from?
- What stories belong to my Country?

These questions led to conversations with Elders that CB had previously avoided. Not because Elders weren't available—but because CB felt unworthy of their time. "Why would they want to teach me? I don't know anything."

Program staff helped bridge this gap. They organized intergenerational gatherings where young people and Elders could be present together without pressure. Over time, relationships formed naturally.

CB learned that cultural responsibility isn't earned through knowing everything—it's accepted by being willing to learn. An Elder explained: "You're already a leader for your cousins, whether you know it or not. They watch what you do. Now you need to learn what to show them."

This reframing was transformative. CB wasn't catching up to where they should already be—they were stepping into a role that was waiting for them. The responsibility wasn't a burden but a gift.

Today, CB actively participates in cultural activities and is beginning to pass on what they've learned to younger family members. The cycle of cultural transmission, interrupted by removal and disconnection, is being repaired one relationship at a time.
